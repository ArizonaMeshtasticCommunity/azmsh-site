MeshMonitor Automations — settings walkthrough

Each block below mirrors a section of MeshMonitor's Automation tab, filled with the values from Will Curran's (Will See on Discord) Arizona home-base instance, and his reasoning beside it. Copy the values as a starting point, then tune for your node and role.

▶ Watch Will's walkthrough on YouTube ⤓ Power users: the full Auto-Ack Cookbook Everything on this page is drawn from the video; watch it once and the settings below will make a lot more sense.

Read this before you copy anything. These are one operator's settings for one specific node (a busy Router-Late home base). They are not a checklist to switch on wholesale. Every automation here transmits, and every transmission is shared airtime for the whole city. Read the use-case for your node and role, turn on only what serves it, and start conservative. A few over-eager MeshMonitor instances can flood a mesh; the goal is a healthier mesh, not a louder one.

The Auto Acknowledge section includes a ready-to-paste trigger pattern and reply templates, plus a short guide to customizing them for your own node.

Will's notes

When he started, he wished someone had DM'd him to say "you're online, you're working." This gives newcomers that moment, and an opening to reply and start a conversation.

Will's notes

The killer feature. Every new operator wonders "is anyone hearing me?" Meshtastic's own ack/retry status is confusing and sometimes wrong.   • All channels active, because people get confused about where to test.

  • Skip incomplete nodes: off. People need to test before you have their full name.
  • Cross-streets in the reply matter for static/city nodes: people want to know how far they're reaching.
  • The reply advertises Auto Ping, so testers know the next step.

Customizing Auto Acknowledge

Read before editing

The pattern and templates above are a working starting point. Here's how to bend them to your node without breaking them.

1. The trigger pattern, piece by piece

The trick is that the four keywords get different strictness, because people use them differently. "test" is unambiguous on a mesh, so it can fire anywhere. "check" and "ping" show up constantly in normal talk ("check this out", "trying to ping someone"), so they only fire in one position.

PieceRule in plain language
(?i)Case-insensitive for the whole pattern. "Test", "TEST", "test" all match.
\b(test|testing)\bLoose. Anywhere in the message: "test", "test 123", "New antenna test", "test at 5th & Main".
^[^0-9A-Za-z_]*ping\bStrict. Only when the message starts with ping (leading emoji/punctuation allowed). "Ping from downtown" fires; "trying to ping someone" doesn't.
\bcheck[^0-9A-Za-z_]*$Strict. Only when the message ends with check. "Radio check", "distance check" fire; "Check this out" doesn't.
\bradio[^0-9A-Za-z_]+check\bRescues "radio check" mid-sentence ("radio check from the truck"), which the end-anchor above would miss.
2. Adding or removing trigger words

Each keyword is its own alternative, separated by |. Decide whether a new word is safe to fire anywhere (add it to the loose group) or needs a position (copy the ping or check shape):

Add "hello" as a loose word: (?i)\b(test|testing|hello)\b|^[^0-9A-Za-z_]*ping\b|\bcheck[^0-9A-Za-z_]*$|\bradio[^0-9A-Za-z_]+check\b Drop "check" entirely (too many false hits in your area): (?i)\b(test|testing)\b|^[^0-9A-Za-z_]*ping\b Prefer a strict, whole-message rule (quieter; won't answer "New antenna test"): (?i)^\s*(?:(?:test(?:ing)?|(?:radio\s+)?check)(?:\s+\d+(?:\s+\d+)*)?|ping)\s*[!?.]*\s*$

The third one is a legitimate alternative if your channel is chatty and you'd rather miss the occasional "Mobile test" than answer a stray one. Will's version came out ahead when backtested against a month of real Arizona traffic; pick based on your own channel's habits.

3. RE2, not JavaScript regex

MeshMonitor compiles the pattern with RE2 for safety. That means no lookahead, lookbehind, or backreferences; exclusions have to be expressed as anchors and character classes (which is why the pattern is shaped the way it is). Inline flags like (?i) are supported. ^ and $ mean start and end of the whole message.

An invalid pattern fails silently. If it doesn't compile, the rule just never fires and nothing in the UI tells you why. So:

4. Always use the Pattern Testing box

Before saving, type your trigger words (fire), the same words inside a sentence (quiet), and a couple of real messages from your channel history. If anything's wrong, fix it before it goes live; a bad pattern on a busy channel replies to everyone. If you have database access, the Cookbook linked below shows how to backtest a pattern against every message you've ever received without transmitting a thing.

5. Writing the reply templates
TokenBecomes
{LONG_NAME} / {SHORT_NAME}The sender's node name
{NODE_ID}Their node ID (hex)
{HOPS}Hops from them to you
{SNR} / {RSSI}Signal of the packet as received by your node. Meaningful only when {HOPS} is 0 — on a relayed packet it grades the last relay's link, not the sender's, so leave it out of the multi-hop template.
{TIME} / {DATE}When you received it
{NODECOUNT}Nodes currently active in your database

Guidelines that keep replies useful: include where you are (cross-streets or a landmark), because "how far did I reach?" is the question people are really asking. Keep it under ~200 characters so it fits a single LoRa packet. On the multi-hop template, drop SNR/RSSI entirely: they describe the last relay's link, not the sender's, and reporting them misleads the tester.

6. Turning SNR into words

"SNR −7.25 dB" means nothing to someone who just unboxed a node, so Will's reply translates it. The bands aren't arbitrary: each is the LoRa demodulation floor for the MEDIUM_FAST preset (SF9/BW250, −12.5 dB) plus a round amount of margin.

SNRWordMargin above floor
≥ +5 dBExcellent~17.5 dB
≥ 0 dBStrong~12.5 dB
≥ −5 dBGood~7.5 dB
≥ −10 dBFair~2.5 dB — still works; rain or foliage will take it
< −10 dBMarginal< 2.5 dB — the next bit of weather drops the link

Steps of 5 dB on purpose: a single packet's reported SNR moves by more than that between transmissions, so finer bands are false precision. If you run a different preset (LongFast has a lower floor), shift the whole ladder. RSSI is printed, not interpreted: below the noise floor it isn't comparable between radios, so turning it into a word would be inventing information.

HopsTapback glyphNote
0*️⃣Keycap asterisk, not 0️⃣: it reads as "direct," not "zero."
1–61️⃣ … 6️⃣Literal hop count.
7+7️⃣Clamped; Meshtastic's hop limit maxes at 7.
unknownnoneTapback skipped and the hop line omitted. Telling a range-tester they were direct when you don't know is a lie.

The words and the distance line ("📍 3.2 mi away") come from a small Python script the automation runs before it sends, with every fragment carrying its own separator so a missing value disappears cleanly instead of leaving "📶 SNR dB". The full script, the automation JSON graph, and the setup steps are in the Cookbook. If you don't want the script, drop the quality word and distance from the template; the rest works on MeshMonitor's built-in tokens.

7. Channel vs DM, and why the two delays

Always respond via Direct Message: on. The hop-count tapback still lands in the channel they tested on, so they get instant confirmation; the wordy reply goes to their DMs and stays out of primary. Will actually runs it as three automations with identical internals: one for a dedicated test channel (no trailer), one for every other channel (reply ends with "please run further tests on the test channel"), one for DMs. That nudges casual testing off the busy channels.

The 10 s delay before the tapback lets the sender's own radio finish its transmit/ACK cycle; replying instantly collides with it and produces NAKs. The 30 s delay before the DM keeps your two transmissions out of the same airtime window. On a busy channel that's the difference between a helpful station and one everybody mutes. Leave Skip incomplete nodes off so people can test before you've learned their full name.

8. Keeping it from flooding the mesh

An auto-responder that answers another auto-responder is the classic way to take a mesh down. What protects you, and what doesn't:

GuardWhat it actually stops
Self-origin drop (engine)Your own node's packets never trigger your rules. A direct self-loop is impossible.
Per-node cooldown 60 sOne reply per sender per minute. Bounds a spammer, and bounds a bot-vs-bot exchange to one round per minute.
30 s spacingAirtime discipline, not loop prevention.
Not protected: somebody else's botIf a neighbour's auto-reply contains one of your trigger words, you answer it, and if yours contains theirs, they answer you. The cooldown limits it; it doesn't stop it.

Two habits that actually prevent the loop: keep your trigger words out of your own reply text (write "run further tests", since \btest\b doesn't match "tests"), and watch your run log for bot-shaped replies from other stations; if one keeps showing up, add a "message does not contain" condition for its prefix. And know that the cooldown lives in memory and resets when MeshMonitor restarts: a crash-looping container has no rate limit at all. The cooldown is a courtesy to your neighbours, not a safety mechanism.

Why this shape

Will's first pattern matched all four words anywhere in a message, which is why it answered him when he said he was "checking something out." The fix wasn't to make everything strict; it was to notice that "test" is unambiguous on a mesh and "check"/"ping" aren't, and treat them differently.

  • Loose on test: people write "New antenna test" and expect an answer.
  • Strict on ping and check: they're ordinary English words.
  • "Radio check" gets its own rule because the strict check-rule would miss it mid-sentence.
  • The plural "tests" in the trailer is deliberate: it keeps your own reply from being a trigger.

Derive your own cut-offs from your own history rather than copying blindly. Read every message a new rule would stop answering by hand; a percentage hides the two genuine tests you just broke.
